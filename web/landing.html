DataSentry inspects the data your pipelines produce, not whether they ran. It flags the change nobody wrote a test for, traces it to the SQL that caused it, and shows who reads the broken numbers — with evidence you can check.

What a passing job doesn’t check

A job status tells you the SQL ran. It says nothing about whether the rows it wrote are the rows you meant. These are three of the nine failures DataSentry’s test harness injects into a synthetic warehouse. Every one of them exits zero.

JOIN_SEMANTICS

A join that fans out

Job status
Success
dbt tests
42 of 42 passed
What happened
A new join to addresses matched customers with two addresses twice. Their revenue doubled.

Caught by the volume and drift detectors, then traced to the join by the semantic diff of the SQL.

UNIT_CHANGE

Cents became dollars

Job status
Success
dbt tests
42 of 42 passed
What happened
An upstream team started sending dollars. The model still divides by 100, so every amount is a hundred times too small — and still a valid number.

Caught by the distribution detector: the quantiles moved, the schema didn’t.

NULL_FLOOD

A third of regions went blank

Job status
Success
dbt tests
42 of 42 passed
What happened
A source API began returning null for region. Nobody had put not_null on that column.

Caught by the null-rate detector, which knows this column’s normal rate from its own history.

The inspection line

Every column passes the same six stations, in this order. Statistics decide what is unusual. A reasoner explains it, but only with evidence it can cite. Ordinary, deterministic code decides what happens next.

      Run the whole line on your laptop

      Python 3.11 and nothing else. It builds a sixty-table warehouse, breaks it six ways, and catches what it can in under three minutes. A free Groq or Gemini key makes the reasoning a language model’s; without one, a deterministic reasoner does the same job.
